A stranger, on a phone, with four seconds

Every landing page is written by someone who already knows what the product does, for someone who does not, has never heard of you, and is one thumb-flick from leaving. Almost every failure on a landing page is a failure to hold that asymmetry in mind.

The one-job rule

A landing page has exactly one job. Not one primary job among several. One.

The test is a sentence: "This page exists to get a stranger to ___." Book a call. Join the waitlist. Buy the $49 tier. Download the template. If the blank needs an "and" or an "or" to fill in, the page is going to underperform on every option in the list, because a visitor asked to choose between two actions frequently chooses neither.

The most common violation is not two buttons. It is a page trying to serve two audiences — a product page that also wants to impress recruiters, a service page that also wants to sell a course. Both audiences read copy addressed to someone else and conclude, correctly, that it is not for them. Split the page. Pages are free.

What a cold visitor actually is

The distinction that matters is not "traffic" versus "no traffic" — it is temperature. A warm visitor arrives already knowing who you are and what you sell; the page only has to close them. A cold visitor arrives from a link, on a phone, mid-scroll, with no context and no reason to extend patience.

  • They do not know what category the product is in, so they cannot fill in gaps from experience.
  • They will decide whether this is relevant to them in a few seconds, and that decision is mostly made from the headline and the first image.
  • They are on a phone, so "above the fold" is roughly one headline, one subhead, and one button.
  • They assume, by default, that this is like everything else they have already dismissed today.
  • They will not scroll to find out what it is. They scroll only after deciding it might be relevant.

Two cheap tests catch nearly all of this. The five-second test: show the page to someone unfamiliar for five seconds, take it away, and ask what it does and who it is for. The squint test: blur the page until text is unreadable and check whether the visual hierarchy still points at one headline and one button. Both take minutes and both find problems that months of self-review will not.

Above the fold: five elements, in order

  1. A specific headline naming what it does, who it is for, and the outcome. This carries most of the page's weight.
  2. One subhead sentence explaining the mechanism, plus whatever qualifier keeps the wrong people out.
  3. One primary call to action, an action verb plus an object, visible without scrolling on a phone.
  4. One risk-reducer or proof element directly beneath the button — a price, a guarantee, "no card required", or a genuine number.
  5. One visual of the actual thing. A screenshot of the real interface, the real garment, the real document. Not a stock photo of a team pointing at a laptop.

Everything else on the page — the manifesto, the founder story, the feature grid — belongs below that. Not because those things do not matter, but because they are answers to questions the visitor has not asked yet, and answering unasked questions is how a page loses someone in its first screen.

Specificity beats adjectives, every time

The single highest-leverage edit available on most pages is deleting adjectives and replacing them with nouns, numbers, and verbs. Adjectives are claims anyone can make. Specifics are claims only you can make.

Adjective versionSpecific versionWhy it works
"Streamline your back-office operations""Turn a billing inbox into drafted invoices before the end of the day"Names the input, the output, and the timeframe
"The best trading journal app""Log a trade in 15 seconds. Works offline. No account."Three checkable claims instead of one unverifiable superlative
"Premium quality apparel""Heavyweight tees, printed after you order, measurements published per size"Facts a competitor selling thin blanks cannot copy
"Powerful analytics dashboard""See which loads are missing a proof of delivery right now"One concrete question the visitor already has"
"Trusted by industry leaders""Founding-client pilot, three slots"True, checkable, and creates real scarcity instead of fake status

There is a rule that catches almost all of it: if a competitor could put your headline on their page without lying, it is not a headline. "Fast, reliable, and easy to use" passes that test for every product ever made, which is why it converts nobody.

The same principle governs the qualifier. "For small freight brokerages running under 500 loads a month" loses you every visitor who was never going to buy and gains credibility with every visitor who fits. Naming your audience narrowly feels like turning away business. It is the opposite: it is the fastest way to make the right person feel spoken to.

Social proof when you have none

The advice to "add testimonials" is useless for a product that has not launched. The temptation is to manufacture them, and that is worth naming plainly as a bad idea: fabricated reviews, invented testimonials, and logos of companies that are not customers are a legal exposure under advertising rules in multiple jurisdictions, and separately they are the thing most likely to be found out by exactly the person you wanted to impress.

The workable substitutes, roughly in order of strength:

  1. Show the work. A live demo, an interactive sample, a public repository, a real screenshot with real data in it. Demonstration outranks description and outranks testimony.
  2. Be specific about something checkable. A number you can defend, a method you can describe, a document someone can open. Specificity itself reads as evidence.
  3. Be transparent about the stage. "Founding-client pilot, three slots, $999 setup plus $249 a month" is credible precisely because it admits earliness. "Trusted by thousands" from a brand nobody has heard of is not.
  4. Reverse the risk. A refund policy, a no-card trial, cancel-anytime, or a pilot with a defined exit. Every one of these converts better than a testimonial from a stranger.
  5. Borrow credibility that is genuinely yours. Published work, a real employer, the standards you follow, the stack you built on. Real and checkable, never implied.
  6. Put a real name and a real face on it. A human being who can be looked up is meaningful proof that someone is accountable for the thing.
  7. Describe the process. "Week one: we connect the inbox and map your invoice formats" tells the visitor you have thought this through further than they have.

And then, from the first real user, ask for one sentence. One specific sentence from a named person beats five generic ones, and beats a logo wall entirely.

Objections as page sections

The most reliable way to structure everything below the fold is to stop thinking about it as content and start thinking about it as a queue of objections, answered in the order a real person raises them.

The objectionThe section that answers itWhat fails
"What is this, exactly?"Three steps, plain language, ideally with a visualA feature list, which assumes the category is already understood
"Is this for someone like me?"Who it is for, and explicitly who it is not forClaiming it is for everyone, which reads as for nobody
"How does it actually work?"The mechanism, briefly, with the real interface shownVague talk of technology, which reads as evasion
"What does it cost?"The price, on the page, as a number"Contact us for pricing", which loses most of the visitors it filters
"What if it does not work for me?"Guarantee, refund policy, or a defined pilot exitSilence, which the visitor fills in pessimistically
"Who are you?"A short founder block with a real name and faceA stock photo and the word "team" when there is one person
"What do I have to do?"The first-week timeline, step by stepLeaving onboarding effort unquantified, so it is assumed to be large
"Is my data safe?"A short, honest security and privacy noteBoilerplate, or nothing at all

The "who it is not for" block is the one people resist and the one that most reliably raises conversion. Ruling people out is what makes ruling people in believable. A page that says "not right for you if you run fewer than 20 loads a month" has just told every visitor above that line that the claims are calibrated rather than aspirational.

Section order should follow the objection order, not a template. And the page should be exactly long enough to answer the objections that exist for that price point — a $19 download needs far less page than a $999 engagement, because the amount of reassurance a visitor requires scales with what they are risking.

One CTA, repeated

The same action, in the same words, in three to five places down the page: in the hero, after the how-it-works section, after the objections are handled, after pricing, and in the footer. Not because visitors miss it, but because different visitors become ready at different points, and the CTA needs to be within reach at the moment readiness arrives.

  • Use identical wording every time. "Start the pilot" in the hero and "Get in touch" at the bottom read as two different offers, and the visitor has to work out whether they are the same. That work costs conversions.
  • Verb plus object. "Join the waitlist", "Book the call", "Get the template". Not "Submit", not "Learn more", which is the button people press instead of the one you wanted.
  • One secondary action at most, visually subordinate and lower-commitment — a sample, a spec sheet, a demo. It exists to catch people who are not ready, not to compete for the ones who are.
  • Reduce the form. Every field costs conversions. If the follow-up is by email, do not ask for a phone number. Name and email is usually the entire form.
  • Say what happens next, right under the button. "I reply within a day" removes more hesitation than another paragraph of copy.

Speed and phones are conversion features

A landing page is mostly text and a couple of images. There is no good reason for it to be slow, and slow pages lose visitors before they read anything — the visitor who leaves during load is invisible in every metric you have. Build it as a static page, inline the critical styles, compress the images, and be extremely suspicious of anything that delays the headline from rendering, particularly a hero video or a font that blocks paint.

Then check it on an actual phone, on cellular, not on a desktop browser resized to look like a phone. That is where the traffic is, and it is where the four-second decision gets made. Measuring it without a tracking stack

You do not need a tag manager, a session recorder, and a heatmap tool. At the traffic levels a new page actually gets, those tools mostly produce confident-looking noise. What you need is one number and enough structure to attribute it.

the only metric that matters early: conversions per 100 sessions not pageviews, not time on page, not bounce rate

Instrumenting it for free

  • Use a distinct destination per CTA placement. A different anchor, a different form URL, or a different mailto subject line for the hero button versus the footer button. Where the click came from is then inferable from the destination, with no JavaScript at all.
  • Use a distinct link per channel. One link in a post, another in a newsletter, another in a bio. Which channel produced conversions matters much more than which button colour did.
  • Put a hidden source field in the form, or a source parameter in the booking link. One field, filled automatically, answers most attribution questions.
  • Count sessions with something lightweight — server logs or a privacy-friendly counter. You need the denominator, not a behavioural profile.
  • Ask every single inbound lead what made them reach out. Twelve qualitative answers are worth more than a dashboard with no conversions in it, and they will tell you which objection the page is failing to answer.

The sample-size honesty section

This is where most solo landing-page optimisation goes wrong. At a 2% conversion rate, the numbers you see at small volumes are dominated by chance. Here is the range you would expect purely from randomness, with a true 2% rate:

SessionsExpected conversionsNormal range from chance aloneWhat you can conclude
10020 to 5Nothing at all
500104 to 16Very little
1,0002011 to 29A large difference, maybe
5,00010081 to 119A real difference, if it is a big one

Read the first row carefully: with a genuinely good page and 100 visitors, getting zero conversions is entirely normal. Redesigning after that is not optimisation, it is reacting to noise, and it destroys more pages than bad copy does. The discipline is to hold the page still until there is enough traffic for the number to mean something.

Which leads to the rule that follows from the table: only test big changes. A different headline and a different offer might be detectable at a thousand sessions. A different button colour is not detectable at any volume you will reach this year. Spend the effort on the headline, the offer, and the objection sections, in that order.

A pre-publish checklist

  1. The one-job sentence is written down and contains no "and".
  2. The headline is a claim a competitor could not truthfully make.
  3. There are no adjectives in the headline or subhead that survive a "prove it" question.
  4. The primary CTA is visible without scrolling on a phone.
  5. Price, or the reason there is no price yet, appears on the page as a number.
  6. There is a "who this is not for" statement.
  7. Every proof element is true and checkable, and nothing is invented.
  8. The same CTA wording appears at least three times.
  9. The form asks for the minimum, and the button says what happens next.
  10. Someone unfamiliar passed the five-second test.

The uncomfortable summary

Most landing pages do not fail on design. They fail because they were written for someone who already understands the product, they ask for two things instead of one, they make claims a competitor could copy word for word, and they get redesigned after a hundred visitors on the strength of two conversions that were always going to be noise.

One job. One audience. Specifics instead of adjectives. Objections answered in the order they arrive. The same button, repeated. And then enough patience to let the number mean something before touching it again.

FAQ

Quick answers

What is the most common landing page mistake?

Asking for two things instead of one. A page has exactly one job, and the test is whether the sentence 'this page exists to get a stranger to ___' can be completed without an 'and' or an 'or'. The subtler version is serving two audiences at once, where both read copy addressed to someone else and conclude it is not for them.

What belongs above the fold?

Five things in order: a specific headline naming what it does, who it is for, and the outcome; one subhead explaining the mechanism; one primary call to action with a verb and an object; one risk-reducer or genuine proof element; and one image of the actual product rather than a stock photo. On a phone that is roughly all the space there is.

How do you write a headline that converts?

Replace adjectives with nouns, numbers, and verbs, then apply one test: if a competitor could put the same sentence on their page without lying, it is not a headline. 'Fast, reliable and easy to use' is true of every product ever made. 'Log a trade in 15 seconds, offline, no account' is three checkable claims.

How do you add social proof with no customers?

Do not invent it, since fabricated testimonials and customer logos are both a legal exposure and the thing most likely to be found out. Substitute in order: show the work with a live demo or a real screenshot, be specific about something checkable, be transparent about the stage such as a founding-client pilot with defined terms, reverse the risk with a refund or trial, and put a real name and face on the page.

How long should a landing page be?

Exactly long enough to answer the objections that exist at that price point, and no longer. A $19 download needs very little page because the risk is small, while a four-figure engagement needs every objection handled, because the amount of reassurance a visitor requires scales with what they are risking.

Should the same call to action be repeated?

Yes, three to five times down the page, in identical wording every time. Different visitors become ready at different points, so the action needs to be within reach when readiness arrives. Changing the wording between placements reads as a different offer and forces the visitor to work out whether they are the same thing.

How do you measure a landing page without analytics tools?

Track conversions per 100 sessions and nothing else early on. Use a distinct destination per call-to-action placement and a distinct link per channel so attribution is inferable without JavaScript, add a hidden source field to the form, count sessions with server logs or a lightweight counter, and ask every inbound lead what made them reach out.

How much traffic do you need before changing a landing page?

More than most people wait for. At a true 2% conversion rate, 100 sessions produces anywhere from 0 to 5 conversions purely by chance, so zero conversions on a good page is entirely normal at that volume. Around 1,000 sessions a large difference becomes visible, which is why only big changes such as the headline and the offer are worth testing at all.
