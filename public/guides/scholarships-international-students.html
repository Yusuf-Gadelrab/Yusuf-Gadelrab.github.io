Eligibility first, essays second

Most of the effort international students put into funding is wasted before it starts, on programmes they were never eligible for. Filter hard on eligibility, then run the rest as a pipeline with deadlines.

Eligibility rules vary by programme and by immigration status, and they change. Nothing here is legal or immigration advice. Verify every requirement directly with the programme and with your school's international student office or academic advisor before you rely on it, and before anything you receive could affect your status.

Where eligibility actually breaks

Read the eligibility section before the prize amount. The overwhelming majority of scholarship databases are built for domestic students, and the clauses that disqualify international applicants are usually one line buried below the description.

ClauseWhat it usually meansWorkaround
"Must be a US citizen or permanent resident"Hard stop, no exceptions, typically federal or federally derived fundingNone — skip it
"Must be eligible to work in the US"Depends entirely on your status and the nature of the awardCheck with your international student office
"Must be a resident of [state]"Residency for tuition purposes, which international students rarely holdUsually a stop; occasionally defined loosely
"Open to students at [university]"Institutional funds — often the friendliest categoryApply; these are the highest-yield ones
"Requires FAFSA"Federal aid, which international students generally cannot file forCheck whether an institutional alternative form exists
"Must be enrolled full time"Common and usually already true for youVerify it stays true across the funded period
"Open to all students regardless of citizenship"Explicitly inclusive — rarer and worth prioritisingPrioritise these

Institutional funding is where international students win most often. Departmental awards, college-level scholarships, endowed funds tied to a major, and research or teaching assistantships are administered by your own university, are frequently silent on citizenship, and have a fraction of the applicant volume of national competitions.

The funding types are not interchangeable

People use these words loosely, and the differences change both who is eligible and how you should apply.

  • Scholarship — money toward education, usually merit or need based, paid to you or applied to tuition. Most citizenship clauses live here.
  • Grant — money for a specific purpose or project rather than for you generally. Often need-based when governmental, but project grants are judged on the project, which is a much better fit if your work is strong.
  • Fellowship — funding attached to a programme, cohort, or research position. Frequently includes a stipend and sometimes constitutes employment, which is exactly where immigration status becomes a real constraint rather than a checkbox.
  • Assistantship — paid work as a research or teaching assistant. This is employment; whether you can hold it depends on your status and the work authorisation rules that apply to you.
  • Startup credits and non-cash awards — cloud compute, API credits, software licences, hardware. Rarely citizenship-restricted because they are not payments, frequently generous, and almost nobody applies because they do not feel like "funding".
  • Competitions and hackathon prizes — prize money is treated differently from employment income in many contexts, but not in all of them. Verify before you accept.

The category most consistently underused by international students is non-cash credit programmes. Free compute, credits, and tooling do not carry the citizenship clauses that block cash awards, and for anyone building projects they remove a real constraint immediately.

Building the pipeline

Funding applications fail on logistics far more than on merit. Deadlines are scattered across the year, each application wants slightly different materials, and recommendation letters take weeks. Treat it as a pipeline with a tracker, exactly like a job search.

  1. Build the list first, apply second. Spend one session finding thirty candidates before you write a single essay. Sources: your university's financial aid and departmental pages, your major's professional societies, company and foundation programmes, and community or heritage-based funds.
  2. Filter on eligibility immediately and record the reason for every rejection so you never re-read the same programme in three months.
  3. Record the deadline type. Hard-deadline and rolling programmes need completely different behaviour: rolling ones reward applying early, hard ones reward planning backwards.
  4. Work backwards from each deadline — letters requested four weeks out, transcripts two weeks out, essay drafted two weeks out, submission three days early. Portals fail on the final day, predictably.
  5. Batch by material type. Write all the 500-word essays in one sitting, request all letters in one sitting. Context switching is what makes this feel enormous.
  6. Track status and outcome so next year starts from a working list rather than from zero.

Volume matters more than polish here, within limits. Award rates are low and largely outside your control, so twelve solid applications beat three immaculate ones. The exception is any programme with a named contact or an interview stage, where effort per application genuinely converts.

Recommendation letters

  • Ask four weeks ahead, minimum. Two weeks is an imposition and it shows in the letter.
  • Ask people who have seen you work, not people with impressive titles who taught you once.
  • Send a packet with the request: the programme description, the deadline, your CV, and three specific things you would like mentioned. This is a favour to them, not presumption.
  • Ask one person to write for several programmes at once, and give them the full list up front.
  • Send a reminder a week out and a thank-you afterwards with the outcome. You will need them again.

Reusing one essay without it reading as generic

You cannot write twelve original essays and you should not try. The workable method is a core narrative plus a fitted opening and closing — the middle is genuinely reusable because it is your actual story, and the parts that make it feel tailored are short and cheap to rewrite.

REUSABLE CORE (~70% — write once, edit rarely) · The specific problem or moment that started this · What you built, studied, or did about it — with a concrete detail · What it cost and what you learned that you did not expect · Where it is going next FITTED OPENING (~15% — rewrite per application) · Names the programme's stated priority in its own words · Connects it to the first line of your core in one sentence FITTED CLOSING (~15% — rewrite per application) · What this specific funding changes about what happens next · A concrete plan, not gratitude

Two rules make the difference between reuse and laziness. First, the closing must be specific about what the money changes — "this scholarship would let me reduce work hours and finish the study I started in the lab" is an argument; "this scholarship would help me achieve my dreams" is filler. Second, the concrete detail in the core has to be genuinely concrete. A number, a name, a moment. Generic essays are not generic because they were reused; they are generic because they contain nothing that could only have been written by one person.

  • Mirror the programme's own stated values in their own words, once, in the opening. More than once reads as pandering.
  • Answer the question that was actually asked. A large share of applications are eliminated for answering a different prompt.
  • Respect the word limit exactly. It is the cheapest possible way to lose.
  • Cut every sentence that would be true of any applicant.
  • Have someone who does not know your work read it and tell you what you do. If they cannot, the essay is not doing its job.

One more time on eligibility

Some awards carry conditions that interact with immigration status — how a stipend is classified, whether an assistantship counts as employment, what a taxable prize means for you. These are not questions to resolve by reading a forum. Your school's international student office answers them for free and answers them for your specific case.

The mechanics of work authorisation itself are in the CPT vs OPT guide and the Visa Navigator, and the pipeline discipline that makes any of this work is the same as Apply OS.

FAQ

Quick answers

Can international students get scholarships?

Yes, but narrower. Institutional and departmental funding, private foundations, and citizenship-neutral programmes. Verify eligibility per programme.

Where does eligibility break?

Citizenship or permanent residency clauses, state residency, and FAFSA requirements. Read eligibility before the award amount.

Scholarship vs grant vs fellowship?

Scholarship funds your education, a grant funds a project and is judged on it, a fellowship attaches to a position and may count as employment.

What gets overlooked?

Non-cash awards — compute, API credits, software, hardware. Rarely citizenship-restricted, often generous, and almost nobody applies.

How do I run the pipeline?

List thirty first, filter on eligibility, log hard vs rolling deadlines, plan backwards, batch by material, submit three days early.

How do I reuse an essay?

Reusable core of ~70%, rewritten opening and closing per programme. Say what the money specifically changes, and keep one detail only you could write.
