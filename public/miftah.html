Miftah Foundation

مفتاح

The key is free.

Miftāḥ is Arabic for the key. Four programs, each designed to hand someone a key to a door that was locked: a first line of code, a form nobody explained, a block nobody sweeps, a database nobody checked.

Where we actually are

Miftah Foundation is not incorporated. No application for federal charitable tax exemption has been filed with the IRS and nothing is pending, so nothing given to it is deductible on anyone's return — and nothing is being asked for. There is no board and no staff: one person, four program designs. $0 raised. $0 spent. Zero programs run.

Everything below describes what is planned. The full ledger — what is filed, what is spent, what remains — lives on the transparency page, and it will stay published whatever happens next.

Mission

One sentence, and every word of it is a commitment, not a report.

Remove the cost barrier to opportunity: teach computer science in a learner's own language, help students navigate the systems that decide their futures, restore neglected public space, and secure the technology of nonprofits that cannot afford to protect themselves.

Miftah Foundation, stated mission. No program has run yet.

The name is the promise. A key is worthless to the person who already has one and priceless to the person locked out — so the key has to be free, or it is just another gate with a nicer sign on it.

Why This Exists

I have been on both sides of the language barrier in a computer science classroom.

Arabic and English are both first languages in my house. That sounds like an advantage, and in most rooms it is. In a computer science classroom it quietly is not — because a beginner is already holding four unfamiliar things at once (the syntax, the mental model, the error message, the tool), and being handed all four in your second-strongest language adds a fifth. The concept was never the hard part. The translation tax was.

I now teach on the other side of that. Since August 2024 I have been a CS tutor and curriculum lead with SJSU's Coding Warriors and the CS department, and I run CS programming at Yerba Buena High School through the university, with expansion to Lynbrook underway. The same thing happens in every room: a student who reasons fluently about a problem out loud goes silent the moment they have to reason about it in a language they are still acquiring — and everybody in the room, including the student, reads that silence as "not a coder."

That is not an anecdote I need you to take on faith. As an undergraduate researcher in Dr. Ethel Tshukudu's CS Education Research Lab at SJSU, I worked on an IRB-approved, mixed-methods study of exactly this with 60 participants. It found statistically significant pre-to-post gains in programming confidence, computing identity, enjoyment and motivation — with novices gaining significantly more than experienced programmers. I am the seventh of eight authors on the paper that came out of it: Exploring Bilingual Coding for Inclusive Computer Science Learning, SIGCSE TS 2026, doi:10.1145/3770761.3777339. One paper. It is the only one I have, and it is real.

So CodeBridge is not a hunch, and the other three programs are the same shape: each one is a thing I already hit, built into something the next person does not have to hit. Passage exists because I spent years navigating student-immigration paperwork and scholarship eligibility rules that nobody explains and specialists charge by the hour to decode. EcoImpact exists because public space in San José is not maintained evenly and the volunteer response to that almost never leaves a record anyone can use. Shield exists because I write an open-source security scanner, and the organizations holding the most sensitive data in this city are the ones least able to pay anyone to look at it.

And that is the whole honest case. None of it has happened yet. What exists today is a design, four working tools, one peer-reviewed paper and one person. What does not exist is a corporation, a board, a dollar, or a single student served. Both halves of that sentence are on this site in the same type size, because a founder who will not say the second half out loud should not be trusted with the first.

Everything in the paragraphs above is checkable: the paper has a DOI, the scanner is public, the tutoring is a role I hold. Nothing here is a Miftah Foundation result, because Miftah Foundation has no results.

The Flywheel

Why this is one organization and not four hobbies.

Each program is both an intake and an outlet for the others. A student could enter anywhere on this loop and keep going. That is the whole design — and it is still a design.

  1. Step 1 · CodeBridge

    A student learns to code in their own language

    The barrier was never the logic. It was being asked to learn two hard things at once.

    then ↓ the skill needs somewhere to go

  2. Step 2 · Passage

    The same student gets scholarship and career navigation

    So the skill turns into a degree and a job instead of stalling at a form nobody explained.

    then ↓ they need proof they can do the work

  3. Step 3 · Shield

    They run a supervised security audit for a real nonprofit

    Real client, real scope, real report. A portfolio piece, a résumé line, a reference letter.

    then ↓ and service hours, with no prerequisite at all

  4. Step 4 · EcoImpact

    Local service hours, logged and quantified

    The lowest-barrier entry point in the foundation. Anyone can show up to a cleanup on day one.

    then ↺ back to the top

↺ And the loop closes: they come back as a CodeBridge mentor, teaching in the language they were taught in. CodeBridge is Shield's labour supply. Shield is CodeBridge's proof of competence. Passage is what keeps a trained student in school and funded. EcoImpact is the door that needs no key at all.
